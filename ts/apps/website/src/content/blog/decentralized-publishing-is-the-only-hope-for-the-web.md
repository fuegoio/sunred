Twenty years ago you kept a short list of sites you trusted: a blogger you liked, a friend's project page, maybe a cartoonist. You checked them by hand, and then RSS arrived and the checking became automatic. New posts landed in a reader, in the order they were published. Nobody ranked them. Nobody watched you read them. If a site published something, you saw it.

Then the opinions moved indoors. Twitter and Facebook solved real problems that personal sites made you handle yourself: hosting took work, and finding readers meant begging strangers for links. The platforms took both jobs, and the price looked small. It was not. They kept the audience, the archive, and the relationship. You published, and they decided who saw it.

Twenty years into that arrangement, we know how it went. Your feed is no longer a feed. It is a slot machine stocked with whatever keeps you scrolling, interspersed with posts from people you never asked to hear from. Influence became a business, the business rewards volume, and the loudest voices win while everything else sinks. We do not think this is what anyone wanted. We think it is what happens when a middleman owns both ends of the conversation.

The way back is not a new protocol or a new startup. It is the web's own structure: individual websites, syndicated over open formats. That system never stopped working.

## RSS is the future of the web

RSS makes a promise no platform would dare make. A feed lists everything a site publishes, in order, in a format any software can read. It carries posts, not engagement metrics. Its terms cannot change next quarter, because there are no terms. The promise is easy to verify: if the post exists, it appears in the feed.

The format asks for nothing in return. No permission, no API key, no rate limit. RSS has been the open door on the web for two decades, and it still is.

People say it is dead, but it carries nearly every podcast on earth. Every podcast app is an RSS reader, and people happily pay for those. A lot of people also kept a personal website, and with it an RSS feed.

Others worry they will miss things without an algorithm. Algorithms miss things too, and they never tell you what. A chronological feed puts the tradeoff in plain sight: you can see what you missed and decide what to do about it. That is the difference between an arrangement and a trick.

## Host your content on your infrastructure

The website holds the other half. It gives a writer a permanent address and full control of the archive. The design answers to the writer, not to a template shared by millions of accounts. A profile lives inside somebody's product. A website stays the source of record even if every app around it disappears.

People keep announcing new federation protocols, and some of them are good ideas. But a protocol is not a home. Whatever identity and messaging standards emerge next, the content itself should live on websites with real addresses, served to whoever asks.

Put the two together and nobody sits in the middle. The writer owns the publication, the reader owns the software, and neither can be throttled or evicted. This is federation in its oldest form. Publish once, and let readers, apps, terminals, and scripts each decide how to present it.

## Why we built Sunred

We do not think the web is finished. We think it has been mismanaged by companies that profit from the mismanagement, and that the pieces needed to fix it have existed all along: pages with stable addresses, feeds that accept any reader, archives owned by their authors.

So we built the reader we wanted to use. Sunred shows new posts from the sites you choose, in chronological order, with nothing in between. It is open source, and you can host it yourself. It will not recommend anything. It will not tell you what is worth your time. It brings the web to you, and you decide what to do with it.
